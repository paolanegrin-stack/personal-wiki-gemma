# Project Initiation: Charter, Scope, Complexity, and Stakeholders

## 1. Start with a clear definition

A project manager carries responsibility for delivery but may not directly control all the people and resources needed. The lecture compares the role to a small CEO. Written agreements help the manager gain support, set boundaries, and coordinate the organization. The planning sequence moves from a high-level **project charter** and **statement of work (SOW)** to deliverables, a work breakdown structure (WBS), task sequence and estimates, resource allocation, risks, and a detailed plan.

## 2. Project charter: authority and direction

The **charter** is issued or approved by the sponsor. It formally authorizes the project and its manager, provides high-level requirements, and gives the manager a basis to commit resources. It also serves as a shared target throughout the project. The slides suggest a short document of about two to five pages containing:

- Project title, brief description, reason for doing it, and link to strategy.
- Specific objectives, expected benefits, results, and major deliverables.
- Assumptions that must later be checked; high-level scope and boundaries.
- Major milestones and timeline; effects on existing systems or teams.
- Roles and contact details; initial funding, people, and other resources.
- Main risks and responses; success measures and targets; approval signatures.

Objectives should be **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. A useful objective states the result and how success will be tested in a concise sentence. For example, a system launch target can include a date, a user-satisfaction target, and a defect limit. If sponsorship, scope, or approval is unclear, the manager should work with leadership to complete the charter before launching the work.

## 3. Statement of work: detailed boundaries

The **SOW**, also called the project scope statement in these slides, develops the charter's high-level scope into a more detailed description. It says what the team will deliver and do, what it will **exclude**, which dates cannot move, and which assumptions support the estimates. A useful scope checklist covers the objective, deliverables, milestones, technical requirements, acceptance criteria, limits, exclusions, and customer review.

An **“is / is not” list** makes ambiguous boundaries concrete. In the slides' IT upgrade example, training *is* two online sessions for IT operations staff; it *is not* training for all employees or contractors or integration with the company training portal. This protects against **scope creep**: gradual growth in requirements, specifications, or priorities without a matching decision about time and cost.

## 4. Responsibilities, priorities, and complexity

A **responsibility matrix** lists activities against the people involved. It clarifies who does the work, who supports it, where teams must coordinate, and what authority each person has. The illustrated market-research matrix marks **R = responsible** and **S = supports/assists**. A RACI matrix is a related way to clarify roles, but the detailed example in these slides uses R and S rather than all four RACI labels.

Projects balance **scope or performance, time, cost, and quality**. Their priorities differ. A fixed event date may force a team to reduce scope; a product launch might value faster market entry. A priority matrix asks which goal to **constrain** (keep fixed), **enhance** (improve if possible), or **accept** (allow some flexibility). This gives the manager a rule for decisions when requirements change or work needs to be accelerated.

The slides also recommend mapping three forms of **complexity**: structural (size, team, tasks, and dependencies), sociopolitical (stakeholder interests, organizational relationships, and conflict), and emergent (new technology, changing methods, and uncertainty). Mapping them helps the team plan resources, anticipate risks, and choose an appropriate management approach. An inventory-control system linking sales representatives, distributors, drivers, warehouses, and possibly retailers across Europe illustrates these interacting demands.

## 5. Stakeholder management is continuous

A **stakeholder** is a person or group that affects the project or is affected by it. Stakeholder satisfaction is one measure of success. People may support the project, oppose it, or remain neutral; overlooking an important group can create serious risks. The process has three repeating steps:

1. **Identify:** List people and groups with an interest, influence, or exposure to the outcome. Internal examples include the sponsor, manager, team, finance, legal, IT, purchasing, safety, and quality. External examples include customers, users, regulators, vendors, communities, and activists.
2. **Prioritize:** Map stakeholders by influence or power and by interest. Give particular attention to those with high influence and high interest. Where useful, define weighted criteria, score stakeholders, and check the result with common sense.
3. **Understand and engage:** Learn what each key stakeholder needs, how they may respond, and how they prefer to communicate. Match the level of contact and participation to their impact and role. Revisit the map as people and priorities change.

An engagement promise should be precise. **Inform** means providing updates. **Consult** adds listening and feedback on how input affected the decision. **Involve** means working together so concerns shape options. **Collaborate** gives advice and recommendations a stronger role. **Empower** means implementing the decision made by the stakeholder group. Do not promise more influence than the project can provide.

Keep a **stakeholder register** with name, role, contact details, communication needs, project needs, and influence. Privately think through each key person's contribution, authority, goals, possible threats or opportunities, and desired perception of the project. Sensitive assumptions about motives belong in working analysis, not a widely published register.

**Key idea:** Gain a sponsor's support, write a measurable objective and clear scope, assign responsibilities, map complexity, and build trust with stakeholders early. Continue these conversations throughout the project.
