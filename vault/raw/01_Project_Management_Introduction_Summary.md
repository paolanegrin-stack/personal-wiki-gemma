# Project Management: Introduction

## 1. What is a project?

A project is temporary work that creates a unique product, service, or result. It has a beginning and an end. Designing a new car, building a software system, opening a new market, and writing a term paper are projects. Repeating the same manufacturing process or entering daily sales receipts are ongoing operations. A project should have a clear objective, a delivery date, and an understanding of the resources or budget available.

For example, a manager might ask a team to fix a system by December 20 with up to five people. Before starting, the team needs to define what “fix” means, how success will be measured, what work is included, and whether the people and funding are enough. An assignment becomes manageable when its goals and constraints are clear.

## 2. Why project management matters

Projects are increasingly common and complex. Products change faster, knowledge grows quickly, customers expect more, and organizations must consider people, profit, and environmental impact. Work that is unique often brings uncertainty about staffing, estimates, execution, and control. Project management helps an organization assign responsibility and authority, coordinate people, and turn a goal into action.

Projects can fail even when they appear to have a clear goal. The slides use examples from aviation and an AI health care startup to show that technical problems, weak training, safety risks, logistics, and poor implementation can undermine an initiative. One historical benchmark cited in the lecture, the Standish Group's 2014 CHAOS report, classified 32% of projects as successful, 44% as challenged, and 24% as failed. These figures are a dated illustration, not a current success rate. They show why a project needs more than a promising idea.

## 3. How to judge success

The direct goals of a project are **performance or scope, cost, and time**. They affect one another: adding features may require more money or time; reducing the schedule may increase cost or reduce what can be delivered. The delivery date and cost are part of the result, not just administrative details. Teams should decide what outcome is needed and what trade-offs are acceptable.

Meeting the original schedule and budget is useful, but it is not the whole story. The lecture also encourages teams to measure outcomes: benefits, value created, impact, opportunities, and risks. A completed system that nobody uses, for example, has not achieved its purpose.

## 4. The project life cycle and core tools

The course follows four broad stages:

1. **Initiation:** Explain the need, select and prioritize projects, identify stakeholders, and establish a project charter and statement of work.
2. **Planning:** Break the work into parts with a work breakdown structure (WBS), assign roles with a responsibility or RACI matrix, estimate the budget, develop the schedule, and allocate resources. Scheduling tools include Gantt charts, PERT, CPM, and methods for dealing with limited resources.
3. **Execution and control:** Carry out the work, manage risks, monitor progress, communicate results, and adjust when conditions change. Risk tools include qualitative maps and quantitative analysis.
4. **Termination:** Audit, close, and present the project and its results.

The course places particular emphasis on planning. Its cases cover different settings, such as IT, construction, product development, and organizational change. Standards and organizations mentioned include the Project Management Institute (PMI) and PRINCE2.

## 5. Link projects to strategy

Organizations use projects to make changes and carry out strategy. A well-run project can therefore create a competitive advantage. Before approving one, managers should ask how it supports the organization's goals and whether it is a better use of scarce people and money than other proposals.

- A **project** delivers one unique result.
- A **program** groups related projects that share a goal, dependencies, or a common timeline and budget. Managing them together can produce benefits that the individual projects could not deliver alone. A major event, for example, may require venue, transport, and event-management projects.
- A **portfolio** groups projects, programs, and sometimes operations to achieve strategic goals. Portfolio management selects priorities and moves resources toward the work that adds the most value.

## 6. Practical principles

A strong project needs an active executive sponsor, a clear reason to exist, and a connection to a larger goal. The project manager should understand the technical work while enabling the team to perform well. Motivated people matter as much as formal processes. Because uncertainty and changes are normal, managers should assess risks early, remain flexible, and update plans when new information arrives.

**Key idea:** Define the intended result, its success measures, its limits, and its strategic purpose before committing people and money. Then manage the work and the changes needed to reach that result.
